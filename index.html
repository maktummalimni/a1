Where our name comes from

The Mackinaw: a blanket that became a coat

A Mackinaw is a short, heavy coat made from thick, densely woven and felted wool — often in a bold red-and-black or green-and-black check. It's named after the Straits of Mackinac, the waterway between Lake Michigan and Lake Huron.

As the story is usually told, the style began in the early 1810s, when a shortage of winter coats in the Great Lakes region led to heavy wool blankets being cut and sewn into short jackets. They were warm, tough and shed snow — exactly what people working outdoors needed.

  1. Early 1800sWool trade blankets are cut into short, practical coats around the Great Lakes.
  2. Later 1800s"Mackinaw cloth" becomes the name for this thick, water-resistant wool.
  3. Early 1900sLoggers, surveyors and outdoor workers adopt the style, adding big bellows pockets.
  4. TodayIts spirit lives on in wool shirt-jackets, overshirts and chore coats.

We added "Petal" because good outerwear should also feel soft, personal and a pleasure to wear.

Fit & sizing

Measure once, buy with confidence

Size labels vary from one maker to the next, but measurements don't. Wrap a soft tape around the fullest part of your chest, under your arms, keeping it level and snug but not tight.

  • Shoulders: the seam should sit at, or just past, the edge of your shoulder bone.
  • Sleeves: with arms relaxed, the cuff should reach your wrist bone and cover a shirt cuff.
  • Length: casual jackets usually end at the hip; coats for real cold should cover your seat.
  • Movement: reach forward and overhead — the back shouldn't pull tight and the hem shouldn't ride up far.

Anatomy of a jacket

The details that separate good from great

  1. Storm flap — a strip of fabric over the zipper that stops wind and rain getting through.
  2. Two-way zipper — opens from the bottom too, so you can sit, cycle or drive comfortably.
  3. Baffles — the stitched compartments that keep insulation evenly spread.
  4. Adjustable cuffs — hook-and-loop tabs or snaps that seal out cold air.
  5. Hem drawcord — cinches the bottom to keep warmth in on windy days.
  6. Taped seams — sealed stitching; essential on any truly waterproof jacket.
  7. Hand-warmer pockets — lined, set high enough to use with a backpack hip belt.
Care by fabric

Keep it looking good for years

Most jackets are washed too often and dried too hot. Always check the label first — then use these fabric-by-fabric habits.

  • Brush after wear with a soft clothes brush to lift dust before it settles into the fibers.
  • Air it out overnight instead of washing — wool naturally sheds odor.
  • Spot-clean with cool water; blot, don't rub.
  • If the label allows washing, use cold water, wool detergent and dry flat.
  • Wipe with a barely damp cloth; never soak or machine-wash.
  • If it gets wet, let it dry slowly at room temperature, away from radiators.
  • Condition once or twice a year, testing a hidden spot first.
  • Store on a broad hanger in a breathable garment bag, never plastic.
  • Wash only when needed, in a front-loading machine with a down-specific wash.
  • Rinse twice to remove all soap, which can clump the down.
  • Tumble dry on low with clean tennis or dryer balls until fully dry — this can take hours.
  • Store loosely, not compressed in its stuff sack.
  • Dirt and skin oils reduce breathability, so wash shells more often than you'd think.
  • Use a technical cleaner; skip fabric softener, which clogs membranes.
  • When water stops beading, refresh the DWR finish with a wash-in or spray-on proofer.
  • Many finishes respond to gentle heat — check whether the label allows a low tumble.
  • Wash inside out, in cold water, to slow fading.
  • Wash rarely if you want raw denim to develop personal fades.
  • Line dry to avoid shrinkage and stiffness.
  • Waxed cotton: never machine-wash; sponge with cold water and re-wax when it looks dry.
Care-label decoder

What do those little symbols mean?

Tap a symbol from your jacket's label to see what it's telling you. The five basic shapes are a tub (washing), a triangle (bleaching), a square (drying), an iron (ironing) and a circle (professional cleaning).

Glossary

Outerwear words, explained simply

Baffle

A stitched or walled compartment that holds insulation in place.

Boiled wool

Knitted wool that has been shrunk and felted to become dense, warm and wind-resistant.

Buffalo check

A large, bold two-color check, classically red and black.

Chore coat

A boxy, hip-length work jacket, traditionally cotton, with patch pockets.

Cruiser

A longer wool work jacket with many pockets, linked to forestry work.

DWR

Durable water repellent: a surface finish that makes water bead and roll off.

Fill power

A measure of down quality — higher numbers mean more loft and warmth for the weight.

Gabardine

A tightly woven twill fabric that resists wind and light rain; classic on trench coats.

Gusset

An extra panel, often under the arm, that adds freedom of movement.

Hydrostatic head

A waterproofness rating in millimeters — how tall a column of water fabric can hold back.

Melton

A heavily fulled, smooth-faced wool cloth used in pea coats and many Mackinaws.

Overshirt

A heavyweight shirt worn as a light jacket; sometimes nicknamed a "shacket".

Placket

The strip of fabric around a front opening that holds buttons or snaps.

Raglan sleeve

A sleeve that runs in one piece to the collar, giving easy shoulder movement.

Yoke

A shaped panel across the shoulders or back that helps a jacket hang well.

FAQ

Questions we hear most

What is a Mackinaw jacket?

A Mackinaw is a short, double- or single-breasted coat made from thick, dense wool, often in a bold check pattern. It's warm, wind-resistant and sheds light snow, which made it popular with people working outdoors in the Great Lakes region and beyond.

Is wool or down warmer?

For the same weight, down is warmer in dry cold. Wool is heavier but keeps much of its warmth when damp, breathes well during activity and resists wind when densely woven. Many people own one of each.

How often should I wash my jacket?

Far less than most people think. Wool and leather rarely need washing at all — brush, air and spot-clean them. Rain shells benefit from more regular washing, because dirt reduces breathability. Always follow the care label.

What's the difference between water-resistant and waterproof?

Water-resistant fabrics shed light rain for a while, usually thanks to a tight weave or a DWR finish. Waterproof jackets use a membrane or coating and taped seams to keep out sustained rain.

How much room should a jacket have for layers?

As a rough guide, look for a garment chest about 4 inches (10 cm) larger than your body over a shirt, about 6 inches (15 cm) over a sweater and about 8 inches (20 cm) over a heavy knit. Our chest calculator does the math for you.
